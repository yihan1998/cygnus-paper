Modern cloud services such as web search, e-commerce, and serverless computing operate under stringent microsecond-scale latency service level objectives (SLOs) \cite{barroso2017attack,kaffes2019shinjuku,marty2019snap,zhang2021demikernel,iyer2023achieving}.
Meeting both mean and tail end-to-end latency SLOs are critical for service providers, not only for maximizing service throughput but also for ensuring a responsive user experience.
Achieving these latency SLOs depends on \textit{how compute resources are scheduled to jobs} within each server, particularly under high load and job size variability.

Prior works \cite{prekas2017zygos,kaffes2019shinjuku} demonstrate the necessity of preemptive scheduling and many works \cite{demoulin2021idling,iyer2023achieving,lin2024fast} follow the trail of optimizing preemption mechanisms to achieve microsecond-scale preemption latency.

However, we believe it is time to rethink not just \textit{how} preemption is implemented, but \textit{which} preemptive scheduling policy to use.
Preemptive policies can be categorized into two classes: \textit{age-based} and \textit{size-based}.
The former prioritizes jobs based on their age (i.e., time spent in the system), while the latter uses job size (i.e., total service time required) or estimated remaining service time to make scheduling decisions.
Age-based policies, such as Processor Sharing (PS) \cite{} and Least Attained Service (LAS) \cite{}, are appealing due to their simplicity and robustness to unknown job sizes.
Size-based policies, such as Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT) \cite{} and Gittins index \cite{}, can achieve optimal or near-optimal mean response times when job sizes are known or can be accurately estimated.
The key distinction between these two classes lies in \textit{when preemption is triggered}: age-based policies typically operate on fixed time slices or aging thresholds, while size-based policies preempt only when there is a change in job priorities.

Prior works \cite{kaffes2019shinjuku} embrace age-based scheduling, in particular, Processor Sharing.
Despite its theoretical appeal, PS faces several limitations in practice.
First, PS is approximated through fixed time slicing, also known as \textit{time quantum}.
The choice of time quantum determines the benefit of PS: large quantum delays short tasks, while small quantum incurs more frequent preemption and thus higher context switching overhead.
