Modern datacenter applications like financial trading systems, video conferencing and social networking operate under extremely stringent, microsecond-scale Service Level Objectives (SLOs).
Meanwhile, these applications exhibit highly diverse workload characteristics, encompassing a wide range of request arrival patterns and service time distributions.

Such diversity in workloads motivates the need to tailor scheduling policies to meet application-specific SLOs.
% Tail latency-sensitive services, such as in-memory key-value stores and web search, benefit from preemptive scheduling to minimize queueing delays. 
% In contrast, interactive applications like e-commerce and social media prioritize responsiveness, often requiring policies like Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT) to minimize mean latency.
% Moreover, soft real-time workloads, including video conferencing and online gaming, operate under strict temporal constraints, demanding deadline-aware scheduling to guarantee frame rates and minimize jitter.

However, enforcing application-specific scheduling policies at microsecond-scale remains to be challenging.
% Traditional kernel schedulers provide abundant scheduling policies but operate at millisecond timescales, unsuitable for modern applications.
% To compensate, recent dataplane operating systems (OSes) bypass the kernel to achieve microsecond-scale preemptive scheduling in userspace.
% Yet they lack the flexibility for applications to define custom scheduling policies.
% Instead, they rigidly hardcode a hybrid policy by combining centralized First-Come-First-Served (FCFS) for light-tailed workloads with Processor Sharing (PS) for heavy-tailed workloads.
 
In this paper, we present \sysname, a dataplane OS architecture that enables applications to customize microsecond-scale scheduling policies without modifying the underlying OS.
Our key observation is that state-of-the-art dataplane OSes conflate scheduling policy and mechanism within the library OS, leaving applications with little authority over scheduling decisions in its own runtime.
To address this limitation, \sysname decouples policy from mechanism:
applications specify scheduling policy, and the dataplane OS enforces it efficiently and safely on bare metal at microsecond-scale.
% Our key observation is that the rigidity in state-of-the-art dataplane OSes comes from the conflation of scheduling mechanisms with scheduling policies.
% Building upon this observation, \sysname aims to truly decouple scheduling policies from scheduling mechanisms:
% application developers solely define the scheduling policy whilst the dataplane OS provides the necessary mechanisms to enforce it at microsecond-scale.
% Our key insight is that the policy flexibility of kernel schedulers originates from the omniscient perception of low-level hardware events.
% The lack of such perception from userspace is the root cause of inflexible scheduling policies in existing dataplane OSes. 
% Therefore, the goal of \kernel is to safely expose low-level hardware events to userspace, thereby enabling applications to build custom scheduling policies directly around these low-level events.

\sysname relies on three key mechanisms to securely delegate scheduling authority back to the application.
First, \sysname allows applications to implement the scheduling policy in \textbf{Policy Engine}.
Second, \sysname enforces application-defined scheduling policies through a trusted scheduler, \textbf{MesoCore}, which runs inside the \textbf{Shadow Ring}. 
Finally, \sysname introduces the \textbf{Interrupt Express} subsystem in the Kernel to securely multiplex low-level hardware between the MesoCore and the Kernel.
