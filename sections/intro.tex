Modern cloud services such as web search, e-commerce, and serverless computing operate under stringent microsecond-scale latency service level objectives (SLOs) \cite{barroso2017attack,kaffes2019shinjuku,marty2019snap,zhang2021demikernel,iyer2023achieving}.
Meeting both mean and tail end-to-end latency SLOs are critical for service providers, not only for maximizing service throughput but also for ensuring a responsive user experience.
Achieving these latency SLOs depends on \textit{how compute resources are scheduled to jobs} within each server, particularly under high load and job size variability.

Many existing works \cite{prekas2017zygos,kaffes2019shinjuku,fried2020caladan,demoulin2021idling,iyer2023achieving,lin2024fast} demonstrate the necessity of preemptive scheduling to meet latency SLOs under both light- and heavy-tailed job size distributions.
One widely adopted scheduling policy is Processor Sharing (PS) \cite{wierman2012tail}.
PS allocates equal service capacity to all runnable jobs, reducing head-of-line blocking for short jobs with the presence of long-running jobs.
Recent systems improve fine-grained preemption via inter-processor interrupts \cite{prekas2017zygos,kaffes2019shinjuku,fried2020caladan}, cache-coherent notifications \cite{iyer2023achieving}, and user-level interrupt mechanisms \cite{lin2024fast}.

However, we argue it is time to rethink not just \textit{how} preemption is implemented, but \textit{which} preemptive scheduling policy to use.
Despite its theoretical appeal, Processor Sharing faces several limitations in practice.
First, PS is approximated through fixed time slicing, also known as \textit{time quantum}: large quantum delays short tasks, while small quantum incurs more frequent preemption and thus higher context switching overhead.
Second, PS is memoryless: it treats all jobs equally regardless of their size, age, or estimated remaining service time.
As a result, long-running jobs may linger in the system for extended periods, delaying newer or shorter jobs and ultimately degrading mean and tail latencies.

We believe that scheduling should be guided by expected benefit, prioritizing tasks based on dynamic reward rather than static time-sharing.
Our approach draws inspiration from the Gittins index \cite{scully2020gittins}, a near-optimal policy for minimizing mean response time ((i.e., queuing + service time)) in $M/G/k$ systems with unknown job sizes.
We adapt this strategy to improve both mean and tail end-to-end latency across light- and heavy-tailed job size distributions.
Unlike fixed-interval preemption, our scheduler defers preemption until a job's priority falls below that of another, avoiding unnecessary context switches.

We present \sysname, a single-address-space operating system that implements dynamic, reward-based scheduling with microsecond-scale preemption granularity.
