Modern cloud services, including web search, online retail, and serverless platforms, operate under microsecond-scale latency Service Level Objectives (SLOs) \cite{barroso2017attack,kaffes2019shinjuku,marty2019snap,zhang2021demikernel,iyer2023achieving}.
Meeting these SLOs critically depends on how compute resources are scheduled, especially under high load and job size variability.
Ineffective scheduling inflates both mean and tail latency, reducing service throughput and degrading the responsiveness of user-facing applications.

Prior studies \cite{prekas2017zygos,kaffes2019shinjuku} demonstrate that preemptive scheduling is essential for meeting the latency SLOs of modern cloud services.
Subsequent works further focus on reducing preemption latency to the microsecond scale.
For example, ZygOS \cite{prekas2017zygos}, Shinjuku \cite{kaffes2019shinjuku}, and Caladan \cite{fried2020caladan} use inter-processor interrupts (IPIs) to achieve preemption latencies of 5-10 µs. Concord \cite{iyer2023achieving} uses cache-coherent notifications to reduce latency to 1-2 µs, while Vessel \cite{lin2024fast} employs user-level interrupts to further lower preemption latency to sub-microsecond levels.

However, beyond solely reducing preemption latency, it is time to reconsider \textit{which preemptive scheduling policy best serves latency-sensitive workloads}.
Preemptive scheduling policies can be broadly classified into two categories: \textit{age-based} and \textit{size-based}.
Age-based policies, such as Processor Sharing (PS) and Least Attained Service (LAS) \cite{}, prioritize jobs using only their service history.
These policies are oblivious to job size and can be implemented efficiently using fixed-interval timer interrupts.
Size-based policies, such as Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT) \cite{} and the Gittins index policy \cite{}, schedule jobs based on known or estimated remaining service time.
These policies provide optimal or near-optimal mean response time but require accurate job size estimation and dynamic priority management, which increases implementation complexity.
These two classes are fundamentally different in \textit{when preemption is triggered}.
Age-based policies are typically \textit{time-driven}, relying on fixed-length time slices (i.e., \textit{time quanta}) to trigger preemption. 
In contrast, size-based policies are \textit{event-driven}, adjusting preemption points dynamically based on changes in job priority ranking.

Prior works \cite{kaffes2019shinjuku,iyer2023achieving,lin2024fast} adopt age-based scheduling due to its simplicity, because it can be implemented efficiently using fixed-length time quantum driven by periodic interrupts.
However, age-based scheduling has two fundamental challenges.
First, age-based preemption is approximated through fixed time slicing, also known as \textit{time quantum}.
The time quantum introduces a trade-off: large quanta delay short jobs, while small quanta increase preemption frequency and context-switching overhead.
Second, age-based policies are job-agnostic: they do not account for job size or remaining service time when making scheduling decisions.
As a result, long jobs may occupy resources disproportionately, delaying short jobs and degrading both mean and tail latency.

We believe that preemptive scheduling should be guided by the \textit{estimated future benefit} of serving each job, rather than retrospective metrics such as age.
Our approach draws inspiration from $\gamma$-Boost \cite{}, a variant of the Gittins index that provides a near-optimal policy for minimizing mean response time (i.e., queuing + service time) and reducing tail latency with unknown job sizes.

We present \sysname, a single-address-space operating system that implements reward-based scheduling policy with a novel \textit{event-driven} preemption at microsecond-scale.
Unlike prior systems that rely on fixed-interval timer interrupts to trigger preemption, \sysname proposes a proactive-reactive preemption model.
In this model, each worker core proactively schedules its own preemption using a local timer via the APIC register, while also responding to reactive preemption from a centralized controller triggered by external events such as network packet arrivals.
