In this section, we analyze the scalability challenges in using a centralized dispatcher to preempt remote worker cores.
We first analyze the overhead of preemption primitives commonly used in existing dataplane OSes, including POSIX signals, Inter-Processor Interrupts (IPIs), and cache-coherent messaging (\S~\ref{subsec:overhead-analysis}).
Based on these empirical measurements, we construct an analytical model to formalize the scalability limits of a centralized dispatcher (\S~\ref{subsec:model-setup}).
We then use the model to evaluate how increasing the core count impacts system performance on preemption delay (\S~\ref{subsec:preemption-delay}) and core efficiency (\S~\ref{subsec:core-efficiency}).

\subsection{Centralized Preemption Model}
\label{subsec:model-setup}

We use an analytical model to illustrate the scalability dilemma of using a centralized dispatcher to preempt worker cores.
We consider a system with $N$ cores in total, where $n_w$ cores are available for worker threads and $n_d$ cores are dedicated to preemption dispatching ($N = n_w + n_d$).
Each worker core must be preempted at a fixed interval, denoted as the preemption quantum $q$.

Two metrics are critical for predictable and bounded tail latency:
\begin{enumerate*}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textit{Preemption delay} ($\Delta$) measures the difference between the ideal and actual preemption time a worker core.
    This reflects the system's ability to precisely preempt worker cores with the target preemption quantum $q$.
    High delay indicates that worker cores may exceed their time slices, resulting in tail latency SLO violations.
    \item \textit{Core efficiency} ($\eta$) is defined as the fraction of cores used for executing application tasks versus total cores.
    Low core efficiency indicates less computational resources are available for applications, leading to increased queuing delays and consequently SLO violations.
\end{enumerate*}.

Even though the centralized dispatcher could have numerous ways to implement preemption policy, the design spectrum essentially trade off between preemption delay and core efficiency.
In the following sections, we focus on three dispatching policies in the spectrum to illustrate this trade-off:
\begin{enumerate*}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \textit{Sequential unicast}, where the dispatcher preempts individual cores one at a time using unicast IPIs to precisely preempt each core,
  \item \textit{Batched multicast}, where the dispatcher preempts all cores at once using multicast IPIs at fixed intervals equal to the preemption quantum
  \item \textit{Partial batching}, where the dispatcher issues preemptions to groups of worker cores via multicast IPIs every preemption quantum
\end{enumerate*}.

We consider the source of the preemption delay ($\delta$) into four components:
\begin{enumerate*}
    \item Serialization ($C_{serial}$): This is the serialization delay waiting for the dispatcher to issue preemptions to preceding cores.
    \item Preemption issuing ($C_{issue}$): The time for the dispatcher to issue the preemption.
    \item Propagation ($C_{prop}$): The time for the preemption to propagate from the dispatcher to the worker core via hardware interconnect.
    \item Preemption receiving ($C_{recv}$): The time for the worker core to receive and handle the incoming preemption
\end{enumerate*}.

\begin{equation}
\delta = C_{serial} + C_{issue} + C_{prop} + C_{recv}
\label{drift-eq}
\end{equation}

We further break down the following costs using our empirical measurements from \S~\ref{subsec:overhead-analysis}:
The serialization delay ($C_{serial}$) depends on the dispatch policy, which we leave to \S~\ref{subsec:preemption-delay} for detailed discussion.
The preemption issuing cost ($C_{issue}$) is fixed at $c_{d}^{u}$ per preemption for unicast policies. 
For multicast policies, it increases with the number of target cores ($n_{target}$) due to underlying hardware serialization, modeled as the sum of a base setup cost $c_{d}^{b}$ and an additional per-core cost $c_{d}^{m}$.
The propagation delay ($C_{prop}$) is a function of $n_{target}$ due to hardware interconnect contention. We denote this as the sum of a base propagation cost $c_{p}^{b}$ and an additional per-core penalty $c_{p}^{m}$. 
The receiving cost ($C_{recv}$) is considered to be a constant $c_{r}$, representing the fixed overhead of interrupt handling and context switching.

\begin{equation}
C_{dispatch} (n_w) = \begin{cases}
c_{d}^{u} & \text{for unicast} \\
c_{d}^{b} + n_w \cdot c_{d}^{m} & \text{for multicast}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
C_{prop} (n_w) = c_{p}^{b} + n_w \cdot c_{p}^{m}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
C_{recv} (n_w) = c_{r}
\end{equation}

We define the core efficiency ($\eta$) as the ratio between the number of worker cores ($n_w$) and the total number of cores ($N$).
Ideally, at the premise of maintaining preemption precision, we want to maximize $\eta$ with minimal number of dispatcher cores ($n_d$).
However, as core counts grow, the preemption delay increases proportionally, potentially violating the target preemption quantum $q$.
To compensate, the system must dedicate more cores to the dispatcher, reducing the number of cores available for applications.
We represent the objective of maximizing core efficiency as follows:
\begin{equation} 
\begin{aligned} 
& \text{Maximize} & \eta & = \frac{n_w}{N} \\
& \text{Subject to} & \delta_i & \le q, \quad \forall i \in {1, \dots, n_w} \\
\end{aligned} 
\label{core-efficiency-eq}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Preemption Delay}
\label{subsec:preemption-delay}
Preemption delay ($\delta$) is the fundamental cause of preemption precision drift in a centralized dispatcher architecture.
Ideally, a worker core would be preempted exactly at the end of its quantum ($q$), resulting in zero drift. 
However, in practice, preemptions are delayed due to software and hardware overheads described in §\ref{subsec:model-setup}.
As these overheads grow with the number of worker cores ($n_w$), preemption precision drift also increases with system scale, causing potential tail latency SLO violations.

Different dispatching policies have varying trades-offs between software-driven serialization and hardware-driven propagation delay.
